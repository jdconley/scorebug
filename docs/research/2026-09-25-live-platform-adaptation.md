# Live-platform adaptation and timing research

Researched September 25, 2026. Scope: published platform engineering, official ingest guidance and maintained implementation sources. Recommendations below are engineering proposals for Scorebug, not assertions about undisclosed platform algorithms or new qualification results.

## Conclusion

Use established sender-side adaptive bitrate control: respond to sustained queue growth and delivered-throughput changes, reduce load promptly, recover cautiously, and preserve headroom. Keep loss recovery bounded, but select its time budget from the application and measured network. The current 1.5-second capture-to-worker-decoder deadline is a Scorebug laboratory setting, not an industry requirement. Reassess it before spending more effort shaving the relay buffer solely to meet that number.

Smooth volleyball motion, reliable clean local recording and stable A/V matter more to this product than conversational latency. Begin publishing at the minimum without a preflight bandwidth test. Measure time to first usable A/V separately from ongoing delay, adaptation response and viewer latency. They are different quantities.

## Concrete numbers we can reuse as reference points

The [OBS RTMP implementation](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/obsproject/obs-studio/master/plugins/obs-outputs/rtmp-stream.c), inspected on the research date, uses a 200 ms queued-video trigger, 1–2-second send-rate estimation windows, and four-second increase timers. Its increase amount is one tenth of the original configured bitrate. These are implementation choices for its RTMP queue. Our start-low and emergency reset-to-minimum behavior remain Scorebug requirements. Do not copy the 200 ms queue threshold onto SRT's buffer timespan: that buffer also contains unacknowledged traffic retained for recovery.

[YouTube recommends roughly 20% bandwidth headroom](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2853856?hl=en). Together with OBS's guidance, this supports testing a conservative 20–25% reserve, including audio, muxing, protocol traffic, retransmission and keyframe bursts. Delivered throughput at the current low rate is not a measurement of unused link capacity.

[Haivision's SRT guidance](https://doc.haivision.com/SRT/1.5.4/Haivision/rtt-multiplier) suggests approximately four times RTT on a fairly good network without burst loss. It explicitly requires considering loss distribution and recovery bandwidth; a burst can need additional headroom. Use this as a starting point per hop, then measure actual retransmission completion and late drops. It does not justify a universal 400, 700 or 1000 ms buffer. The [gateway guide](https://doc.haivision.com/__attachments/8062901/HMG%203.7.3%20User%27s%20Guide.pdf?inst-v=115d9d92-fcf4-4ffa-8395-96226d38ccc5) explicitly separates transport buffering from capture, encoding, decoding and display delay.

[AWS IVS configuration](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/ivs/latest/LowLatencyUserGuide/streaming-config.html) recommends one- or two-second keyframes and documents a startup tradeoff: roughly 3–4 versus 6–7 seconds in its service. Shorter GOPs can increase rendition switching/buffering. These are service-specific startup numbers, not our target or a claim that minimum-profile publication can literally eliminate connection/keyframe delay.

[Apple's live-stream encoding sample](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/VideoToolbox/encoding-video-for-live-streaming) uses real-time encoding, expected frame rate, target bitrate/data-rate limits and explicit keyframe/lookahead controls. It notes encoder implementations support different properties. Its [low-latency conferencing sample](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/videotoolbox/encoding-video-for-low-latency-conferencing) requests a separate low-latency rate-control capability. Test capabilities on the actual phone; simulator failures cannot establish hardware support.

## What our current evidence means

The latest 700 ms run has clean recorded media, approximately 30 fresh fps and passing sampled audio/score checks. Its capture-to-decoder arrival bounds have median lower 1445 ms and upper 1514 ms, while the configured deadline is 1500 ms. Many windows are uncertain, and their controller appropriately declines increases under that contract. The 1000 ms run also has clean media but fails the same timing contract. Neither completes qualified automatic recovery.

There are two separate questions: is the implementation respecting its configured contract, and is that contract appropriate for this broadcast product? The research supports revisiting the second question. YouTube's five-/ten-second viewer guidance cannot simply replace our decoder deadline: it includes additional processing, delivery and playback. A deliberate stage budget is required.

## Proposed next work

1. Define a reliability-first broadcast latency profile before further buffer tuning. Separate encoder delay, phone-to-relay recovery, relay-to-worker transport, worker decode/overlay/output, destination/player delay, and control-report uncertainty. Also track score-event-to-correct-video alignment.
2. Keep the existing 1.5-second test as a strict benchmark. Compare explicitly versioned 2.0- and 3.0-second capture-to-decoder experimental profiles on the same source/fault schedule. These are proposed test points, not platform standards or accepted production defaults. Define report freshness coherently; an older video frame does not necessarily mean the newly received health report itself is stale. Retain bounded queues and loss/stall detection independent of a healthy report.
3. Use queue growth, acknowledged delivered bytes/goodput, RTT trend, SRT recovery pressure and decoder health together. React before extensive loss. Preserve fast downshift, 24 fresh-fps floor, audio, and slow measured recovery. No source supports our exact 5%-over-two-seconds or 10/20/40/60-second backoff as universal standards; these remain testable product policy.
4. Preserve separate adaptation domains: adaptive phone uplink; qualified, stable codec/dimensions at the overlaid platform output; platform-managed viewer renditions. Use documented RTMPS/keyframe/bitrate settings at the final destination. Do not force a transport rewrite just because Meta used QUIC.
5. Compare startup, stalls, source-frame continuity, A/V sync, latency tails, quality/ramp time, oscillation and archive integrity. Retain former failures under their original settings; a different profile requires a fresh run and cannot relabel old evidence.
6. Run the complete physical phone/paired-Watch vertical early. Measure the hardware encoder and radio before optimizing around simulator-only constraints. Then validate the same named profiles on home/cloud workers and real YouTube playback.

No runtime thresholds, production flags or media code changed during this research. The current automated recovery route and physical paired acceptance remain open.
